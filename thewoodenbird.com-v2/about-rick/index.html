How it started

My wife and I walked into a small shop called Wings & Things in Long Grove, Illinois. The owner's big yellow Lab met us at the door, which was treat enough for a long-time Lab lover. Then I saw the wooden ducks. They looked so real I wanted to touch them.

That Christmas I got Bruce Burk's book, Game Bird Carving, and a blank duck body. I read the book, took a class with Richard LeMaster, an early master of the craft, and I was hooked. I carved about 25 ducks before family and career boxed up the paints, tools and books.

When I retired, I believe my wife was worried that without a hobby I would start alphabetizing the spice bottles. She unpacked some of my old carvings and put them on a shelf. Seeing them, I could smell the sawdust and hear my old tools calling my name.

Now you can find me in the workshop most every day. It is a walk-out basement with two sliding doors onto our woods. My assistant, Kava Bean, our fifth chocolate Lab, keeps an eye on the squirrels and deer from her spot under the workbench.

I provide a product that doesn't get returned, thrown out, or sent to Goodwill. My wooden birds will be around long after I'm gone.

Every bird takes two art forms: sculpture and painting. I start with research, because you can't make a duck look real if you don't know what a real duck looks like. I keep thousands of reference photos and work with two screens of them beside the bench.

I usually spend 50 to 80 hours on a bird. My aim is a duck that looks like it just landed on your family room shelf. When someone commissions a bird, I email progress photos all the way through. It's like we are working on the bird together.

From block to bird

Every bird takes 50 to 80 hours. Here are the steps, with a mallard hen to show each one.

  1. Research

    Reference photos show the feather colors, eye placement, and quirks like the tilt of the head.

  2. Block and band saw

    Rick pencils the pattern on tupelo or basswood and cuts it out on the band saw.

  3. Shape and carve

    A heavy grinder shapes the body. Smaller Dremel-type bits carve the feather groups, bill and head.

  4. Texture and burn

    Every feather barb is raised with fine bits and a Detail Master wood burner. Some birds get inlaid primary feathers.

  5. Paint

    Thin layers of acrylic, then hundreds of fine lines that make the feathers look soft.

Three levels of detail

  • SmoothieCarved and painted without texture. Smoothies are usually floating decoys, sometimes called gunners.
  • Partial detailTexture and inlaid feathers on the back, head and tail, like the 3/4 wood duck in the shop.
  • Full detailEvery feather textured and burned, then painted layer on layer. Most of Rick's commissions are full detail.

For new carvers

Getting started in carving

Rick wrote a guide for people who would like to carve a duck but do not know where to start. You do not need a $400 tool: a $40 Dremel will do. These are the basics.

  • ReferenceBooks by J. D. Sprankle and Bruce Burk, reference photos, and a cast study bill for your species.
  • A rough-out bodySkip the band saw and start from a tupelo or sugar pine blank, about $50.
  • A cordless DremelThe Dremel 7300, about $25, does everything you need at first.
  • A micromotorLater, a high-speed micromotor works like a dentist's drill for texture and detail.
  • Dust controlCarving makes a lot of fine dust. Rick's guide shows the setup he uses.
