On the Bench

How a block of tupelo becomes a duck

A bird starts as a block of wood and ends with 5 to 7 layers of paint. Here is how it gets there.

Every bird takes 50 to 80 hours. Here are the steps, with a mallard hen to show each one.

  1. Research

    Reference photos show the feather colors, eye placement, and quirks like the tilt of the head.

  2. Block and band saw

    Rick pencils the pattern on tupelo or basswood and cuts it out on the band saw.

  3. Shape and carve

    A heavy grinder shapes the body. Smaller Dremel-type bits carve the feather groups, bill and head.

  4. Texture and burn

    Every feather barb is raised with fine bits and a Detail Master wood burner. Some birds get inlaid primary feathers.

  5. Paint

    Thin layers of acrylic, then hundreds of fine lines that make the feathers look soft.

Three levels of detail

  • SmoothieCarved and painted without texture. Smoothies are usually floating decoys, sometimes called gunners.
  • Partial detailTexture and inlaid feathers on the back, head and tail, like the 3/4 wood duck in the shop.
  • Full detailEvery feather textured and burned, then painted layer on layer. Most of Rick's commissions are full detail.

For new carvers

Getting started in carving

Rick wrote a guide for people who would like to carve a duck but do not know where to start. You do not need a $400 tool: a $40 Dremel will do. These are the basics.

  • ReferenceBooks by J. D. Sprankle and Bruce Burk, reference photos, and a cast study bill for your species.
  • A rough-out bodySkip the band saw and start from a tupelo or sugar pine blank, about $50.
  • A cordless DremelThe Dremel 7300, about $25, does everything you need at first.
  • A micromotorLater, a high-speed micromotor works like a dentist's drill for texture and detail.
  • Dust controlCarving makes a lot of fine dust. Rick's guide shows the setup he uses.
